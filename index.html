01 Why it matters

The reaction happened to the patient, but the record stays at the hospital.

  • Paper cards and memory

    A reaction history lives only on a paper card and in the patient's memory, so it is lost when the clinic or pharmacy changes.

  • A gap in DUR

    Korea's Drug Utilization Review checks every prescription, but it holds no individual allergy history.

  • One leaflet for everyone

    Each person has different drugs to avoid, yet prescriptions and counseling go out in the same words to all.

What actually happens

A patient who had anaphylaxis to cefaclor is prescribed a cephalosporin antibiotic again at another hospital. The old card said what had happened. It did not say what to avoid from now on.

04 Five tools

Five tools and how they are used

It started with one app in 2017 and grew into a card, hospital-to-hospital sharing and a MyData medication guide. Each tool also says whether you can use it today.

On Google Play2018, Android · MOHW patient safety research

Drug Allergy Alert

WhenYou are allergic to painkillers and cannot tell whether the pharmacy's medicine contains that ingredient

  1. An allergy specialist registers the drugs you must avoid and the drugs you can take.
  2. You search a drug name or scan the QR code printed at the bottom of your prescription.
  3. You see at once whether it is contraindicated, caution or safe to take. If it is contraindicated, you talk to the prescribing doctor.
  4. At the clinic and pharmacy you show the app's drug safety card screen.
What changes for the patient

Registering the NSAID class (ATC M01A) as contraindicated is designed to cover its 120 ingredients plus 28 more from the same class that come as patches, eye drops and gargles.

Digital Drug Safety Card

WhenIt is hard to explain your reaction accurately in an emergency room or a new hospital

  1. A specialist assesses the reaction and sorts out the drugs to avoid, to use with caution and safe to take.
  2. You receive it the way you prefer: a plastic card with a QR sticker, a patient app or a KakaoTalk message.
  3. Show the QR at a hospital or pharmacy and the clinician opens the full record.
What changes for the patient

The old card only said what had happened. This card also shows, like a traffic light, the drugs to avoid and the drugs you can take instead.

Safe medication guide and Yagirang

WhenYou get medicines from several hospitals and do not know what to watch for

  1. Download your own visit and prescription record from the My Health Record app.
  2. Upload it, and a rule engine looks for risky combinations and drugs older adults should be careful with.
  3. AI writes the guide in plain words, and a doctor checks and approves it.
  4. In the Yagirang app you see why you take each drug, how many times a day and for how many days, and which ingredients to watch.
What changes for the patient

It starts from the one file you downloaded yourself, with no link to the hospital's system. Guides pile up by date so you can open them again at any time.

Personal drug allergy guide

WhenYou were diagnosed with a drug allergy but were handed only a generic leaflet

  1. Enter the allergy record, and it works through cross-reactivity to list the classes to avoid, the classes to use with caution and the drugs to use instead.
  2. Every question, such as "What do I tell the pharmacist?", gets an answer and a sentence you can read out as it is.
  3. Once an allergy specialist approves it, you receive it as a PDF and a QR card.

Allergy sharing between hospitals

WhenA doctor at another hospital cannot see your allergy record

  1. Allergy records that each hospital wrote differently are written with the same standard data elements.
  2. They go to the other hospital in the international HL7 FHIR format.
  3. From phase 2, patients could open their own allergy information in the My Health Record app.

05 What fits me

What you can do in your situation

Pick the closest situation to see a first step you can take today. The services named here run in Korea.

My situation

Turn your reaction record into a card you carry

  1. At your next visit, ask for "an adverse drug reaction assessment and a drug safety card". At some hospitals with a Regional Pharmacovigilance Center, such as CBNUH, a specialist sorts out the culprit drug and the drugs to avoid.
  2. Keep the card or app screen with you at all times. A photo on your phone also works.
  3. Show it to the doctor and pharmacist first, every time you get a prescription or buy medicine.
What to say at the clinic

"I had a reaction to ○○ before. Could you list the drugs I should avoid from now on?"

KIDS, adverse reaction reporting and counseling (Korean)

Gather your medicines into one list and have it checked

  1. Download your recent prescriptions at once from the My Health Record app. Drugs from several hospitals end up on one list.
  2. At your next visit, show that list and ask whether the drugs are safe together.
  3. If dizziness, falls, a dry mouth or constipation is new, mention it. It may come from a medicine.
What to say at the clinic

"I brought every medicine, including those from other hospitals. Could you check that they are safe together?"

Health Information Highway, My Health Record app (Korean)

Check your avoid list on your phone

  1. Ask your doctor whether the hospital can register you for Drug Allergy Alert or a Digital Drug Safety Card. Only participating hospitals can.
  2. Once registered, install the app on an Android phone and check your drug safety card.
  3. When you get a new medicine, search its name or scan the QR code on the prescription to compare.
What to say at the clinic

"Could I get my contraindicated and safe drugs on an app or a card?"

Drug Allergy Alert on Google Play

This guidance does not replace medical care. Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before stopping or changing a medicine. For urgent symptoms such as breathlessness or a swelling throat, call emergency services (119 in Korea).
