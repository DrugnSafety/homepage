Whose record is it,
when the side effect was mine?

It starts with the side-effect record and ends with the whole health record in the patient's hands.

I am Min-Gyu Kang, Associate Professor in the Department of Internal Medicine at Chungbuk National University College of Medicine and an allergist at Chungbuk National University Hospital. I have seen patients here since March 2015 and have been an associate professor since September 2023. I am board-certified in Allergy and Clinical Immunology and certified in medical informatics, and I have served as Director of the Regional Pharmacovigilance Center and Director of the Medical Information Center.

A patient who has had an adverse drug reaction is prescribed the same drug again at another hospital. The history lives on a paper card and in memory, so it breaks the moment the hospital or the pharmacy changes. DUR, the prescription check run by HIRA, holds no individual allergy history either. Every patient is different, yet the prescription and the medication counseling are the same.

The body that reacted belongs to the patient, but the record belongs to the hospital, so the patient falls back on memory. Put the information in the patient's hands and everything changes. The patient checks it first, then shows it to the hospital and the pharmacy. When the record follows the person, the prescription fits that person, and the patient takes the medicine with confidence.

The starting point is the side-effect record. From there it widens to allergy information, and in time patients will take their whole health record and use it to manage their health. Then the patient walks into the clinic holding their own chart.

STEP1

My side-effect recordThe side effects I had and the drugs to avoid

This began in 2017 as a patient safety study funded by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, and in 2018 we released the Drug Allergy Alert app (Allimi). Patients check on their phone which drugs to avoid and which are safe to take, then scan the QR code on a prescription to cross-check it. Next came the QR drug safety card, and 709 patients at 9 university hospitals have registered on the My ADR platform.

STEP2

My allergy recordA record that travels between hospitals

For a record to cross from one hospital to the next, the form has to be the same first. With the consortium of Prof. Hye-Ryun Kang at Seoul National University Hospital we set the standard data elements and refined the allergy resource of HL7 FHIR, the specification hospitals use to exchange health information. After 3 hospitals exchanged 914 records, the work moved onto the Health Information Highway and reached 8 hospitals.

When a patient brings a downloaded record, a rule engine and several large language models (LLMs) draft a safe medication guide and the clinical team reviews it. Clinical decision support (CDSS) starts with the patient. Across 3 institutions, 111 patients registered and 108 reports went out to them. They rated their willingness to recommend it 4.95 out of 5.

Five lines of research

A problem I meet in the clinic with a drug allergy patient becomes a standard for sharing information, then a tool in the patient's hand, then a question for pharmacovigilance and clinical research. The answer comes back as a feature of the hospital system.

Drug allergy information sharing and standardization Ongoing

A drug allergy confirmed at one hospital is invisible at the next hospital or pharmacy. A patient who had anaphylaxis to cefaclor really does get the same class of antibiotic again at another institution. The HIRA DUR system does not carry an individual allergy history, and a paper drug safety card is hard for a patient to keep at hand at all times.

Standard form and HL7 FHIR AllergyIntolerance

The culprit drug, the symptoms, causality, and the contraindicated, caution and safe-to-take drug groups all fit on a single standard form (ver1.0, 2022), which we mapped to the FHIR AllergyIntolerance resource (KR Core, FHIR R4, 2024). We also built JSON and Excel conversion tools so implementers can work with the standard resource.

Health Information Exchange program, Korea Health Information Service

With the consortium led by Prof. Hye-Ryun Kang of Seoul National University Hospital, we built a pilot system that shares drug allergy records between hospitals (phase 1, 2023.10-2024.4; phase 2, migration to the Health Information Highway, 2024.8-2025.3). I set the standard data elements and value domains and refined the FHIR AllergyIntolerance profile. The work won an Excellence Award at the 2023 Health Information Exchange best-practice contest.

Digital Drug Safety Card and Allergy Passport

We issue the card in a standard form, and a single QR code opens it at any hospital or pharmacy (6 institutions). We also demonstrated an Allergy Passport that carries the same information into GPT and explains it in other languages. 2024 Korean Society for Patient Safety President's Award.

Patients bring records they downloaded from the Health Information Highway (MyHealthWay) to the hospital, and we connected the Safe Medication CDSS so that it reasons from those records (MOHW R&D 2023-2025). Pharmacoepidemiology and active pharmacovigilance

This line of work turns hospital electronic medical records into a common data model to find drug safety signals, then measures their scale and risk factors with epidemiology.

CDM-based active pharmacovigilance

The Common Data Model (CDM) at Chungbuk National University Hospital began in 2015, when I joined a commissioned study of the Korea Institute of Drug Safety and Risk Management (KIDS) as the site lead. In 2017 I developed the analysis algorithms and a way to link a hospital EMR common data model with the Korea Adverse Event Reporting System (KAERS). Within that same line of work, the Korean multicenter active pharmacovigilance system (K-Sentinel/K-CDM), I took charge of moving hospital data into the standard structure. In 2019 I used the CDM to assess oseltamivir adverse reactions, and in 2020, as Principal Investigator, I ran the project that built a hospital electronic medical record common data model and set the backbone of our in-house CDM. Since then we have served as an analysis site in the KIDS MOA-CDM network (a hospital EHR based common data model, codebook 1.2 to 1.4), feeding our hospital data into multicenter pharmacovigilance analyses. Since 2021 I have led Chungbuk National University Hospital as a participating institution in the Medical Data-Driven Hospital program (MOHW) under the Seoul National University Hospital consortium, rebuilding the CDM on the OMOP standard and widening its domains.

Pharmacoepidemiology studies

Outside the CDM, I have kept measuring the scale and outcomes of adverse drug reactions in real-world data. We surveyed across multiple centers what share of emergency department visits adverse drug events account for and what they cost, and that work produced a series of papers, including a first-author paper in PLOS ONE. We also developed and validated a trigger tool that finds drug-related emergency visits automatically. For severe cutaneous adverse reactions we analyzed a nationwide multicenter registry together with individual case safety reports to map the phenotypes and direct costs of each culprit drug, and I took part in genetic risk factor studies: HLA-B*58:01 pre-screening for allopurinol, HLA-B*13:01 for carbamazepine, and HLA-DRB1*15:02 for iodinated contrast media. The work has since widened to angiotensin receptor blockers and drug-induced liver injury, to oseltamivir, and to spontaneous reporting data on adverse drug reactions in older adults.

Asthma and allergy clinical research

Clinical research that starts with the patients in my clinic. It runs in four branches: thesis research, epidemiology and biomarkers, patient care tools, and clinical trials.

Thesis research: gene expression in asthma and steroid hyporesponsiveness

My doctoral work read the pathophysiology of asthma out of gene expression in peripheral blood mononuclear cells. In the transcriptomes of patients during acute exacerbation I looked for shared gene modules and regulatory networks, and ex vivo dexamethasone experiments identified the molecular signature of poor steroid response. The same line runs on to a 2016 young investigator grant that approached the mechanism of steroid resistance in severe asthma through metabolomics, and to a 2022 Respiratory Research paper that split the immune phenotypes of asthma by peripheral blood transcriptomic clusters.

Patient self-management apps and digital therapeutics

I have spent years building digital tools that let patients manage asthma and allergic disease themselves. It began with a 2013 abstract on a smartphone-based asthma action plan, and in 2016 I reported the feasibility of an app-based action plan and monitoring in Asia Pacific Allergy. In a 2020 planning study for the Korea Health Promotion Institute I designed a smart healthcare service model for chronic respiratory and allergic disease in primary care. From 2021 I served as Co-Principal Investigator on the Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy program to build an AI technology support platform for the wellcare industry. An app that lets patients with a drug allergy answer 'Can I take this medicine?' for themselves.

Drug allergy looks different in every patient. Even within the same NSAID allergy, a drug that is fine for one patient can be dangerous for another, so it is hard for a patient to judge alone at a pharmacy or a clinic. Drug Allergy Alert is an Android app that carries the contraindicated, caution and safe-to-take drugs recorded for a patient at a university hospital allergy clinic, and answers in real time whether a particular medicine is safe. Because it grades risk from each patient's own allergy record, it puts clinical precision medicine in the patient's hand.

Key features

Allergy record entered by the clinician for each patient

Drugs confirmed in the allergy clinic as contraindicated, to use with caution, or safe to take are entered at the ATC code level, together with an action plan for that patient. Every answer the app gives rests on this record.

Mobile drug safety card

The paper drug safety card the hospital used to hand out now sits on the app screen. The culprit drug and the contraindicated, caution and safe-to-take drugs fit on one screen, so the patient only has to show it at another hospital or a pharmacy.

Drug allergy action plan

The rules a patient has to follow are spelled out as numbered sentences. For example: avoid NSAIDs down to patches and eye drops, but celecoxib and acetaminophen are safe to take.

Instant answer by drug name

Search a brand name or an ingredient name and the app answers at once whether that drug is safe for the patient. When the drug is contraindicated, it also tells the patient to talk to the prescribing doctor.

The same drug, a different answer for each patient

A patient who cross-reacts to every NSAID searches ibuprofen and gets a warning that it is dangerous.

For another patient the same drug is safe

A patient who reacts only to the diclofenac class searches the same ibuprofen and is told it is safe to take. That the answer changes from patient to patient is the heart of this app.

Prescription QR code scan

Photograph the QR code at the bottom of a prescription and the app checks every prescribed drug at once for anything the patient is allergic to. UBcare and EDB provided the technical support behind this feature.

Three years spent turning the paper drug safety card into a QR digital card and getting drug allergy information to travel between hospitals in a standard form

A patient who has had a drug allergy or a severe adverse drug reaction meets the same risk again at every new hospital. This project standardized the issuing criteria and the data elements of the drug safety card patients carry, and turned it into a digital drug safety card whose QR code opens the full detail. What we built

Digital Drug Safety Card

We reviewed the existing paper and plastic drug safety cards and the European Drug Allergy Passport, then designed a new form. A QR code sits on the front, and the card shows the culprit drug and the symptoms along with the contraindicated, caution and safe-to-take drugs, like a traffic light. Opening the QR code brings up the full detail, down to causality and severity.

Standard drug safety card form

We settled on two forms: a general one carrying every data element of the Korea Institute of Drug Safety and Risk Management (KIDS) drug safety card, and a dedicated one for contrast media, where allergic reactions are common. Depending on what suits the hospital, a site can choose a form that prints the reconciliation information on the card itself, or a form that carries only the adverse event and sends the rest to the QR code.

Standard form for drug allergy information sharing

We defined the data elements needed to move adverse reaction information between hospitals and mapped them to HL7 FHIR AllergyIntolerance. On top of the onset date, culprit drug, symptoms, severity and causality of the adverse event, we added reconciliation elements as extensions: contraindicated, caution and safe-to-take drug groups. Adverse event names follow MedDRA and severity follows CTCAE.

My ADR platform (myadr.care)

A web platform that carries the standard form as it is. It handles patient registration, culprit drug lookup, and the entry and review of adverse reaction information, and works with KD codes, ATC codes and SNOMED CT codes side by side. A template feature prefills the data elements each institution uses, and from a saved record you can print a PDF card, issue a QR card or send a KakaoTalk alert on the spot. We ran the test server and the service server separately and published the user manual on GitBook.

Patient mobile app and KakaoTalk alerts

Working with Kakao Healthcare, we built an app on the MyData model, where the patient holds and reads their own adverse reaction record. For patients who find apps hard to use, we added a second route that sends the digital drug safety card image and link by KakaoTalk alert.

Passport on Drug Allergy GPT

A generative language model demo with the standard form and the shared data elements written into its instructions. Upload an allergy information file mapped to FHIR and it answers from that file, judging a given drug as contraindicated, caution or safe to take by ATC code. It can also translate the card into other languages, Chinese included.

Rollout led by Regional Pharmacovigilance Centers

Using the Chungbuk National University Hospital workflow as the reference process, we widened participation along two tracks: the Regional Pharmacovigilance Centers and the Korean Society of Health-System Pharmacists. Card issuing differs by hospital, so we wrote down and fixed a process for each site, from putting a QR sticker on a physical card to sending the link by KakaoTalk alert alone. We also piloted a link that fills in the sharing form inside the EMR and posts it to the platform over an API.

Adopted as the standard for a Ministry of Health and Welfare program

The adverse drug reaction sharing standard built in this project was adopted as the standard for the Ministry of Health and Welfare pilot program on a drug allergy information sharing system between institutions. We also proposed adding drug allergy to the allergy and intolerance section of the Health Information Highway, and an upgrade to the allergy elements of the Korean Core Data for Interoperability (KR-CDI) held by the Korea Health Information Service.

What we built

SMART DUR CDSS web app, myadr.care

Uploading the My Health Record FHIR file the patient brings fills in the patient profile, and the clinician works through five tabs: baseline polypharmacy questionnaire, prescribed drugs, comorbidities, adverse drug reactions, and review and send. The prescribed drugs tab organizes the past year of medication by ATC code and shows days of supply next to medication adherence (PDC).

Drug interaction analysis

For every combination of the drugs the patient takes now, the system finds interactions from DrugBank evidence and explains them. The ingredient analysis tab shows each product's approved labeling (dosage, contraindications, adverse reactions) in Korean and in English.

Potentially inappropriate medications and anticholinergic burden

A table lists fall-risk drug classes with their risk level and the drugs whose adherence needs checking. For drugs with a high anticholinergic burden score, the system suggests alternative ingredients by ATC code.

STOPP criteria screening

Working from version 3 of the STOPP/START criteria, we registered 146 rules as diagnosis (ICD-10) and drug (ATC) conditions, so that a patient who meets a condition gets the explanation in patient-facing wording. Clinicians can edit the rules and add their own from the settings screen.

Multi-agent LLM guide generation

A multi-agent LLM turns the analysis into a polypharmacy reconciliation guide the patient can read. You can pick models and compare them on the same input (LLM evaluation), and nothing reaches the patient until a clinician has reviewed and approved it.

Three patient reports

The polypharmacy reconciliation guide (what the current drugs do, drugs needing special care, drugs to keep taking, combinations to watch when taken together, symptoms to look out for, overall recommendations), the Smart Allergy Report (contraindicated, caution and safe to take by drug class, with a Q&A), and personalized medication counseling notes.

Yagirang app

The smartphone app patients use. They receive the reports and the safety card, get reminders and keep a medication log, and write down what they went through after taking a drug (e-PRO) to send back to the study team.

Smart Drug-Allergy Prevention Guide multi-agent LLM

A multi-agent system that takes a patient's own adverse drug reaction history and builds a personalized drug allergy prevention guide automatically. It runs on the OpenAI Agents SDK and is written in Python and Streamlit. Four specialist agents run in a mix of sequence and parallel, with a target of under 3 minutes end to end. Input is a FHIR AllergyIntolerance JSON file or free text, and drug classification refers to a WHO ATC code mapping file.

Results and dissemination

  • We validated the whole path at 3 hospitals: the patient downloads their own record from the Health Information Highway, brings it to the hospital, and the file goes straight into the CDSS analysis.
  • We built a polypharmacy reconciliation CDSS that layers an LLM over a rule-based engine and always routes the result through clinician review. The output is structured JSON, so the reasoning can be traced and checked.
  • We delivered three kinds of patient report to real patients. All three use the same grammar: three tiers of contraindicated, caution and safe to take, with explanations at the drug class level.

  • The limits are just as clear. Electronic medical record specifications differ from hospital to hospital, which restricts integration, and where the EMR cannot be connected the system stays unusable no matter how well the CDSS performs.
  • We could not connect to the Health Information Highway server to server, so sharing data cost considerable time and resources. Program structure in three columns: generate, share, use

The generating hospitals (Seoul National University, Chungbuk National University and Kyungpook National University hospitals) push allergy records through a FHIR API into the Health Information Exchange repository at the Korea Health Information Service, and the primary care clinics taking part in the program read them back through the same API. We built no separate infrastructure: the drug allergy sharing repository and its checking system sit on top of the existing Health Information Exchange repository.

How the consortium split the work

Seoul National University, Chungbuk National University and Kyungpook National University hospitals and the Seoul National University College of Pharmacy developed the standard model and generated the standard records. The SNUH Office of Information, SNUH Venture, Planit Square and BIT Computer built the systems. Chungbuk National University Hospital's share was generating the allergy records and mapping them to KR-CDI and KR Core.

Standard data elements and value domains for drug allergy information sharing

The adverse event area holds onset date, culprit drug and drug code, symptoms and symptom codes, severity, causality and a case description. The reconciliation area holds current status, reaction type, severity, causality, whether confirmatory testing was done, the contraindicated, caution and safe-to-take ingredients or ingredient groups, and the specialist's opinion. Document information carries the institution and the assessor, patient information carries the registered patient, and together these four areas make up the form.

Extending HL7 FHIR AllergyIntolerance

I sorted the elements that existing standards cannot hold into three layers. Layer 1 is HL7 FHIR AllergyIntolerance KR (R4), layer 2 is AllergyIntolerance R6, and layer 3 is the drug allergy information sharing standard model, which defines the elements found in neither standard as extensions. Causality, whether confirmatory testing was done, the contraindicated, caution and safe-to-take ingredients or ingredient groups, and the specialist's opinion are the elements newly defined as extensions.

Building the drug allergy information sharing system

Inside the hospital network, the ADR database and an agent server handle FHIR conversion and resource validation, then send the data through a proxy server in the DMZ to the FHIR server at the regional repository. Patient identification and consent checks run on the MPI of the Health Information Exchange system, and the receiving institutions read the consenting patients' data in their EMR viewer. We ran integration tests on nine scenarios and passed all of them.

Standards-based data generation and quality control

We did not use spontaneous reports as they came in. Allergists re-assessed every case rated probable or higher for causality and moderate or worse in severity, and picked what to share. Of the 914 cases collected in the first program, 39% involved antibiotics, 37% NSAIDs and 9% contrast media; 57% were moderate and 40% severe. Four advisory meetings, with allergists from primary care institutions and community pharmacists, reviewed the sharing screens and their contents.

Symposium and outreach

On March 29, 2024 we held a symposium at Seoul National University Hospital for academics, partner institutions in the region and patient groups. All 46 health professionals who attended said the system needed to be built. On June 21, 2024 we ran a session titled 'Using drug allergy information to improve patient safety' at the spring conference of the Korean Society of Medical Informatics, and we spread the word through the Korean Drug Allergy Research Consortium (KoDARC) newsletter and a patient leaflet.

The second program moves to the Health Information Highway

In the first program clinicians had to obtain consent from each patient themselves, and only the 10 clinics that had modified their EMR could look the records up. The second program switched the sharing route to the Health Information Highway: for patients who had already completed the consent process, records move from the hospital ADR database to the PHR database, and patients pull them up themselves in the My Health Record app and show them to their clinicians. Data generation also widened from tertiary hospitals to institutions at every level of care.

The second program's consortium and how it ran

Eight hospitals took part: Seoul and Gyeonggi (Seoul National University Hospital, Seoul St. Mary's Hospital of the Catholic University of Korea, Severance Hospital, Seoul National University Bundang Hospital), Chungcheong (Chungbuk National University Hospital, Dankook University Hospital) and Yeongnam and Honam (Kyungpook National University Hospital, Jeonbuk National University Hospital). A data generation council and an informatics council ran separately and met fifteen times between August 2024 and March 2025 to align cases across institutions and settle a protocol for managing contrast media hypersensitivity.
